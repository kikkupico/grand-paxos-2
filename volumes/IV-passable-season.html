The translator's preface

If the sea may delay a boat without limit, no rite can guarantee that independent captains reach a verdict in the face of silence. This volume is the logbook of the navigators' guild that ended the despair — not by defying the storm, which cannot be commanded, but by pointing out that the Aegean is not, in fact, an infinitely capricious void.

The logbook was kept by three navigators, Ντουόρκ, Λίντς and Στόκμαγιερ. Readers will recognize one of the navigators from the headlands of Volume II: having helped demonstrate the boundary of what cannot be promised, she returned to show what can.

The logbook is long, technical, and thirty-six columns in length. I have kept its central table, its rites, and its lower-bound arguments; its clock constructions I have summarized.

— The Translator

An unconstrained sea is one of infinite caprice. The navigators observed that winter storms, however long, do end — and that thereafter a rowed skiff crosses the sound in bounded time. A sailing code can be built that never divides the fleet in any gale and reaches a verdict once the calm arrives. The impossibility is not defied. It is out-waited.
Chapter I

Between Calm and Storm

1.1 · The Convoy's Dilemma & The Bounds of the Sea

A merchant fleet of N vessels — heavy round-hulled freighters, supply barks, and armed galleys — lay moored in the circular lantern harbour (the cothon) at the narrow neck of Paxos. Autumn was dying into winter. Hardtack barrels and freshwater casks were running low, and the seasonal gales had kept the fleet pinned behind the breakwater for weeks.

The captains faced an existential choice on which their lives and fortunes hung:

  • SAIL (1): Cast off moorings, raise the yards, and attempt the open sea crossing together as an intact convoy to reach the mainland markets before winter locks the sea.
  • WINTER (0): Unrig the masts, haul the hulls onto the dry beach slips, butcher the pack mules, and commit to winter quarters on Paxos until spring.

Ancient seafaring was strictly a convoy enterprise. A lone merchant galley that loses its steering oar or springs a leak in open water is lost; ships relied on sister vessels for towing, rescue, and repelling pirates. If half the captains put to sea while the other half beach their hulls, the departing ships face doom if a squall strikes, while the wintering crews lose half their fighting strength and provisions. Whatever the captains choose, all sound captains must decide the same. A split verdict is disaster.

Some captains may fail during deliberations, and the navigators distinguished four kinds of maritime hazard, from mildest to worst:

The Four Kinds of Faulty Captain
  • The Stranded Ship (fail-stop / sleeper): follows the code correctly, but runs aground on a shoal, snaps its rudder, or whose crew is felled by marsh fever; it ceases sending skiffs and never replies again.
  • The Swamped Skiff (omission): follows the code, but some message pouches carried by its skiffs are lost in the heavy surf, or flares are missed in the rain.
  • The Mutinous Captain with a Signet (authenticated Byzantine): behaves deceitfully — proposing Sail to some and Winter to others — but cannot forge another captain's unique bronze signet ring pressed into pitch.
  • The Pirate Impostor (Byzantine): behaves arbitrarily with no unforgeable seals, fabricating rumours and impersonating other vessels.

The weather across the harbour was described by two bounds. Δ is the longest time a rowed dispatch skiff takes to cross the water and deliver a message tube. Φ is the most by which one captain's crew can be slower than another: in any span of Φ moments, every sound crew takes at least one step. If both bounds exist and are known in advance, the sea is synchronous, and older rites apply: with signet seals, any number of faults can be tolerated; without them, the generals of Volume III showed N ≥ 3t + 1 is needed. If either bound does not exist at all, the sea is asynchronous, and the curse of Volume II proves that no rite can tolerate even a single stranded vessel.

The navigators studied the seas in between.

1.2 · Two Kinds of In-Between

The first in-between sea is one where a bound Δ exists, but no one knows what it is. The impossibility of pure asynchrony does not apply, because the sea is in fact bounded. But every known rite needs Δ to know how long a captain must wait each round. One could guess some Δ and declare any vessel whose skiff takes longer to be stranded — but guess too small and soon every captain is deemed "faulty", and the decisions of faulty captains need not agree with anyone's. What is wanted is a sailing code with no fixed Δ built in, correct whenever some fixed Δ holds.

The second is one where Δ is known, but the harbour is intermittently tossed by storms, delivering skiffs late or holding them in sheltered coves. A late skiff must not cause its captain to be branded dead. So the navigators introduced the central concept of the series: for each voyage there is an unknown Global Stabilization Time — the arrival of the Passable Season, GST — from which point onward the harbour calms and respects Δ.1

1. The navigators anticipated the objection that no calm lasts forever. Any good rite reaches its verdict within some span L after GST, so the calm need only last until GST + L; they preferred to state the time bounds for each rite rather than burden the definition.
Safety in Every Weather

Separate the requirements into safety — no two sound captains ever hoist conflicting pennants, and none decides an improper action — and liveness — every sound captain eventually decides. A rite that solves consensus whenever Δ holds from some GST onward cannot violate safety even if the sea is completely asynchronous. For a safety violation happens at some finite moment, and there is a continuation of that very voyage in which Δ holds afterwards.

This short observation is the foundational bargain of practical distributed systems: safe always, live eventually. The fleet will never sail on a false calm, nor will half the galleys sail while the other half beach their hulls. The navigators framed each version as a game against the sea. If Δ is known, the sea names Δ and the navigator supplies a rite. If Δ is unknown, the navigator supplies the rite first and the sea then names Δ. If Δ holds eventually, the sea names Δ, the navigator supplies the rite, and the sea then picks the day the calm begins.

Two lessons are drawn from it. First, for stranded ships, swamped skiffs, and mutineers with seals, the fleet size needed in a partially synchronous sea lies strictly between the synchronous and asynchronous cases: a strict majority of sound captains (2t + 1) is exactly what is needed to tolerate t failures. Second, when boats are partially synchronous, signet seals do not help against liars: rogue captains require 3t + 1 vessels with or without seals. In every case, the time to reach a verdict is polynomial in N and Δ once GST arrives.

Chapter III

The Rounds of the Harbour

The navigators first designed their rites in an idealized harbor, the basic round model, and later showed how real seas can imitate it. In the basic model, time passes in numbered rounds that all captains share. Each round has a dispatching part, a receiving part, and a calculation part. Skiffs may be delayed during gales — but from round GST onward, every skiff sent from a sound captain to a sound captain arrives in the round it was sent.

All the rites share a common method. A captain p tries to get the fleet to lock onto some verdict v (Sail or Winter) that he has found acceptable, and decides v once enough captains acknowledge the lock that no different verdict can ever be agreed upon later.

3.1 · Rite One: Stranded and Swamped Ships (N ≥ 2t + 1)

Each captain keeps a set PROPER of values he knows to be legitimate: if all captains initially favored v, only v is proper; otherwise both Sail and Winter are. PROPER begins with his own dawn observation, is attached to every message tube, and grows with every value seen in others' reports.

Rounds are grouped into phases, numbered 1, 2, 3, …, each comprising a three-round trying phase followed by a one-round lock-release phase. Phase k belongs to captain pi where k ≡ i (mod N) — the flagship lantern passes clockwise round the cothon. A captain may moor (the logbook says lock) to a value v at phase k; the mooring means "I believe Flagship k may decide v", and he casts it off only when presented with evidence of a higher phase. A value is acceptable to a captain if he is currently moored to no conflicting value.

Trying Phase k (Flagship pi), first round s = 4k − 3
  1. Round s: Every captain sends Flagship pi the list of verdicts (Sail or Winter) that are both acceptable to him and in his PROPER set.
  2. Round s + 1: If at least N − t captains found some v acceptable and proper, pi picks such a v and broadcasts (lock v, k).
  3. Round s + 2: Anyone receiving (lock v, k) moors to v at phase k (casting off any older mooring) and sends an acknowledgment skiff. If Flagship pi receives at least t + 1 acknowledgments, he decides v, hoists his signal pennant, and continues participating to relay his decision.
  4. Lock-release, round s + 3: Every captain broadcasts his current moorings (v, h). A captain moored to v at phase h who learns of a mooring (w, h′) with w ≠ v and h′ ≥ h casts off his mooring to v.
Lemmas 3.1–3.3

3.1. No two distinct values acquire moorings with the same phase number — the flagship would have to send two conflicting lock slates, which an honest captain never does.

3.2. If some captain decides v at phase k, the first phase with any decision, then at least t + 1 captains moor to v at phase k, and each of them stays moored to v at phase ≥ k forever after.

3.3. Immediately after any lock-release phase at or after GST, the sound captains are moored to at most one value.

Why a mooring to the decided value is never cast off (Lemma 3.2)
  1. Suppose some mooring to v made at phase k is first cast off, without being replaced by a higher one, at lock-release phase l. Then someone was moored to w ≠ v at some phase h with k ≤ h ≤ l.
  2. By Lemma 3.1, h ≠ k, so k < h ≤ l. For w to be moored at phase h, it must have been acceptable to at least N − t captains at the start of phase h — so at least N − t captains were not moored to v.
  3. But t + 1 captains were moored to v through the start of phase l, and (N − t) + (t + 1) > N. Overlapping majorities make this impossible. ∎
Theorem 3.1

In the basic model with stranded or swamped ships and N ≥ 2t + 1, Rite One achieves consistency, strong unanimity, and termination for any set of values.

Consistency: after the first decision, t + 1 captains stay moored to v, so no conflicting value can ever again be acceptable to a quorum of N − t, and no contradictory action is ever decided. Unanimity: if all start favoring Sail, only Sail is ever proper. Termination: take any trying phase owned by a sound flagship that follows a lock-release phase at or after GST. At most one verdict is moored (Lemma 3.3), it has had time to become known to everyone, and there are at least t + 1 sound captains — so an acceptable verdict is locked and acknowledged, and the flagship decides. All sound captains decide by round GST + 4(N + 1).

3.2 · Rite Two: Mutineers with Signet Seals (N ≥ 3t + 1)

Against rogue captains who lie, a flagship may send conflicting lock slates to different quays, so the mooring must carry verifiable evidence. Rite Two modifies Rite One with three maritime safeguards. Each lock slate carries a proof of acceptability: the N − t sealed tablets showing v acceptable and proper. The flagship decides only upon receiving 2t + 1 acknowledgments, of which at least t + 1 come from sound captains. And PROPER grows cautiously: a verdict is recognized only when t + 1 captains vouch for it with their signets. The same lemmas hold, and all sound captains decide by GST + 4(N + 1).

3.3 · Rite Three: Pirate Impostors (N ≥ 3t + 1)

Without signet seals, the navigators simulated them with a relay borrowed from Σρικάντ and Τωυεγ: the echo. Time is grouped in superrounds of two rounds. To announce m, captain p sends (init, p, m, k). Anyone who receives an initial message echoes it to all peers. Anyone who hears echoes from N − 2t captains keeps re-echoing it every round; anyone who hears it from N − t captains formally accepts m from p. This ensures that no sound captain ever accepts a forged message, and that if one sound captain accepts a message, all sound captains accept it within one superround after GST.

Chapter IV

Moorings and Releases

The reader of Volume V will recognize the architecture of Rite One. A rotating leader asks a quorum what values it may safely propose; it proposes only a value acceptable to N − t participants; the participants attach a phase number to the promise they make; they abandon a promise only for one with an equal or higher phase number; and a decision requires enough acknowledgments that any later quorum must contain someone still bound by it. Two different majorities of 2t + 1 participants must share a member.

The Part-Time Parliament of Paxos (Volume V) arrives at the identical geometry for managing its civic decrees. The navigators proved the fundamental truth that all fault-tolerant consensus systems are quorums that overlap in time and memory.

Chapter V

Sailing Without a Chart

Real seas have no shared round numbers. The navigators showed how captains with steady rowing crews (Φ = 1, so every crew counts its own strokes and all counts agree) can imitate the basic round model.

5.1 · When the Calm Comes Eventually

If Δ is known and holds from GST, let R = N + Δ. Each crew groups its strokes in blocks of R. In the r-th group the crew spends the first N strokes sending its round-r dispatch skiffs, one captain at a time, and the last Δ strokes receiving; the round-r deliberation happens at the final stroke. Every slate is tagged with its round number, and slates arriving late are ignored. Once the Passable Season arrives, a block of R strokes is long enough for every skiff to arrive, faithfully imitating the basic model.

5.2 · When the Bound Is Unknown

If Δ exists but is unknown to the captains, the navigators' solution is disarmingly simple: wait a little longer each round. Let the r-th group of strokes contain Rr = N + r strokes. Whatever delay bound Δ the sea actually enforces, every round r ≥ Δ allows sufficient time for all skiffs to arrive — so the fleet imitates a basic-model run with GST = Δ. The navigators' growing sandglasses guarantee that patience eventually overtakes the sea.

Chapter VI

Where the Bounds Come From

The navigators proved that their bounds could not be improved. The proofs are storm stories.

Theorem 4.3

With stranded or swamped ships, steady rowers and partially synchronous waters, if 2 ≤ N ≤ 2t, no t-resilient rite achieves even weak unanimity for two values.

The storm story
  1. Split the fleet into two groups of captains, P and Q, each having at least 1 and at most t ships.
  2. Scenario A: All captains start favoring WINTER (0). Group Q is stranded from the start; skiffs within P take exactly one moment. P must decide within some finite time TA. That decision must be WINTER (0) — otherwise, if Q were alive but its skiffs to P took longer than TA, P would decide Sail while every captain on the island started with Winter.
  3. Scenario B: All captains start favoring SAIL (1). Group P is stranded; skiffs within Q take one moment. Q decides SAIL (1) within some time TB.
  4. Scenario C: Group P starts with Winter (0) and Group Q with Sail (1). Everyone is alive and sound, skiffs within each group cross swiftly in one moment, but a violent storm surge over the causeway delays skiffs between the two shores longer than max(TA, TB). Group P observes identical events to Scenario A and decides WINTER (0); Group Q observes identical events to Scenario B and decides SAIL (1). ∎

When the storm clears, the disaster has happened: Group P has beached its galleys and unrigged its sails, while Group Q has cut its moorings and rowed into the open sea alone. This proves why no protocol can tolerate t failures when N ≤ 2t. A strict majority (2t + 1) is an unyielding mathematical requirement.

Theorem 4.4

With mutineers with seals, steady rowers and partially synchronous waters, if 2 ≤ N ≤ 3t, no t-resilient rite achieves weak unanimity for two values.

The proof splits the fleet into three groups P, Q, R, each of at most t vessels. In Scenario C, Group Q contains mutinous captains who send sealed Winter slates to P and sealed Sail slates to R — their seals are genuine, since they are only sealing their own conflicting messages — while a gale delays all direct communication between P and R. The fleet splits. This demonstrates why 3t + 1 is required whenever Byzantine faults are present in partially synchronous waters, even with cryptographic signets.

Chapter VII

The Drummers of the Headlands

When rowers too are only partially synchronous, no captain can count strokes and assume other crews' counts agree. The navigators therefore constructed distributed clocks — descendants of the monotonic beads of Volume I — out of two kinds of drumbeat across the sound. An i-tick is the signal "i". An i-claim is "I have drummed an i-tick to everyone." Each captain drums ticks, collects claims from peers, and advances his private clock when a quorum of claims is confirmed.

These fault-tolerant drumming protocols ensure that even if some crews are sluggish or mutinous, private clocks never run ahead of real time and advance at a steady rate once GST arrives. The fleet recovers the ability to measure rounds, and the resiliencies of Table I are fully achieved.

Partial synchrony — specifically the GST model — is the foundation of virtually all modern distributed consensus systems. It explains the core architecture of Paxos, Viewstamped Replication, PBFT, and Raft: guarantee safety unconditionally under any network delay, and guarantee liveness once the network stabilizes for long enough. The navigators' growing hourglasses correspond directly to the exponential backoff and timeout extensions used in modern leader elections, and Table I's 2t + 1 and 3t + 1 thresholds are the exact quorum sizes that govern production systems today.
