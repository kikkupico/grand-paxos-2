The translator's preface

For centuries the law of the admirals (Volume III, Traitors Among Admirals) had been correct, celebrated and unusable. It told a city how many honest keepers it needed to survive traitors — more than three for every traitor — but the rites that achieved it either assumed the dispatch skiffs ran on a fixed timetable, or demanded so many sealed orders that the sealing wax cost more than the war.

This volume is the building record of the first Byzantine citadel a working city could afford. Its builders were two inquisitors, Κάστρο and Λίσκοφ — the latter the granary clerk of Volume VI, now building in stone. They did not bring a new theorem. They brought a budget.

The three-for-every-traitor law is not the inquisitors' invention, and they never claimed it was. Their contribution was to make a citadel obeying it cheap — above all by taking the costly seals off the ordinary business of the citadel.

— The Translator

A treasury is kept by 3f + 1 keepers, of whom up to f may be bribed. One keeper, the warden, proposes the order of business; the rest check him in three rounds before anything is done. If the warden stalls or lies, the keepers depose him and the next in rotation takes the keys — without ever forgetting what was done. Messengers may be delayed as long as the enemy likes; the treasury will never be robbed, only slowed.
Chapter I

Why a Citadel

Pirate raids and the honest mistakes of careless clerks were growing more common, the inquisitors wrote, and the city relied more each year on its treasuries and archives, which made raids more attractive and their success more serious. Both a bribed keeper and a keeper who has simply blundered can do anything — lie, destroy records, contradict himself — and a treasury that survives only honest failures is not enough.

There had been a great deal of work on agreement among keepers who might lie. But most of it was either designed to show that agreement was possible in principle and too slow to use, or it assumed synchrony — known bounds on how long messengers and keepers take. The two practical fortifications of the day, the Rampart and the Secure Ring, relied on synchrony for their very correctness. And that, the inquisitors pointed out, is dangerous against an intelligent enemy.

A fortress that must expel keepers it believes faulty, and decides who is faulty by timing them, can be attacked without bribing anyone honest. The enemy slows the honest keepers' messengers until they are declared dead and expelled, and keeps doing it until the bribed keepers are no longer a minority. Such a siege by delay is far easier than corrupting an honest keeper. The inquisitors' citadel does not rely on synchrony for safety, and it never expels anyone. It also runs more than ten times faster than the Rampart.1

1. The Rampart was the work of Ράιτερ; the Secure Ring of Κίλστρομ, Μόζερ and Μέλιαρ-Σμιθ. Both are described in Chapter X.
Chapter II

The Laws of the Citadel

2.1 · The Sea and the Enemy

The citadel stands in an asynchronous sea: messengers may fail to arrive, be delayed, arrive twice or out of order. Faulty keepers may behave arbitrarily. The inquisitors assumed keepers fail independently — and noted that against a real enemy this takes deliberate effort: each keeper should follow a differently written copy of the rules, keep a different password and answer to a different master, so that one trick does not corrupt them all.

Three kinds of mark protect the letters. A signet seal (a public-key signature) proves who wrote a letter to anyone. A split tally (a message authentication code) proves authorship only to the one other keeper who holds the matching half. A wax impression (a digest) is a small, unforgeable likeness of a long scroll. The enemy is very strong — it may coordinate bribed keepers, delay messengers, even delay honest keepers — but it cannot delay honest keepers for ever, and it cannot forge an honest keeper's seal, recover a scroll from its impression, or find two scrolls with the same impression.

2.2 · What the Citadel Promises

Safety

Provided no more than ⌊(n − 1)/3⌋ of the n keepers are faulty, the citadel behaves like a single honest keeper executing one request at a time (linearizability) — however many citizens are dishonest, and however the messengers are delayed.

Liveness

Under the same bound, citizens eventually receive replies, provided the delay of a message does not grow faster than time itself, indefinitely.

The inquisitors are clear about why liveness must lean on some synchrony: otherwise the citadel would solve consensus in a fully asynchronous sea, which the priestesses of Volume II proved impossible. Their requirement is weak — likely to hold in any real sea whose storms are eventually repaired — and it is needed only for progress. A dishonest citizen cannot break the treasury's own rules, because every operation, his included, is observed the same way by all; and since changes to who may use the treasury are observed the same way too, a city can recover from a dishonest citizen by revoking his access.

Why 3f + 1 keepers? The citadel must be able to act after hearing from n − f keepers, since f may be faulty and silent. But the f it did not hear from may merely be slow, and f of those it did hear from may be liars. The honest replies must still outnumber the lies: n − 2f > f, so n > 3f. The bound is the admirals' law of Volume III; the inquisitors cite a later proof that it is also the best possible in an asynchronous sea.2

2. The proof they cite is by Μπράχα and Τωυεγ. The inquisitors also concede that their citadel keeps no secrets: a bribed keeper can tell the enemy what is in the treasury. Sharing secrets among keepers so that no f of them can reveal them is left for another campaign.
Chapter III

The Watch and the Warden

The citadel is a form of the scholar's state machine of Volume I: every honest keeper holds a copy of the treasury and carries out the same requests in the same order. Keepers must be deterministic and start alike. The inquisitors assume exactly n = 3f + 1 keepers, numbered 0 to n − 1; more keepers only add messages without adding resilience.

The keepers pass through a succession of watches (the inquisitors say views). In each watch one keeper is the warden (primary) and the rest are backups. The warden of watch v is keeper p = v mod n: the keys pass round in a fixed rotation. The watch changes when the warden appears to have failed.

The Citizen's Part
  1. A citizen c sends ⟨REQUEST, o, t, c⟩ to the warden, where o is the operation and t a timestamp that grows with each of his requests (his own water-clock will do). This ensures each request is carried out exactly once.
  2. Each keeper replies directly with ⟨REPLY, v, t, c, i, r⟩: watch, timestamp, keeper number and result.
  3. The citizen waits for f + 1 replies from different keepers with the same t and r. At most f keepers lie, so one of them is honest.
  4. If the replies are slow, the citizen sends the request to all keepers. Those who have already carried it out re-send the reply; others relay it to the warden — and a warden who ignores it will be suspected by enough keepers to be deposed.
Chapter IV

The Three-Phase Gauntlet

Each keeper keeps the treasury, a log of the letters it has accepted, and the number of its current watch. When the warden receives a request, it runs it through three phases — pre-prepare, prepare and commit. The first two order requests within a watch even if the warden is lying. The last two ensure that requests which commit keep their order across watches. (Chapter VIII explains how the seals are replaced by tallies; this chapter, like the inquisitors', describes them with seals.)

4.1 · Pre-Prepare: The Warden Proposes

The warden assigns the request m a sequence number n and sends ⟨⟨PRE-PREPARE, v, n, d⟩, m⟩ to the backups, where d is the wax impression of m. The request itself travels alongside rather than inside, so that the pre-prepare is small; this matters, because pre-prepares serve as proof in watch changes that a request was given a number. A backup accepts it only if:

Accepting a Pre-Prepare
  • the seals on the request and the pre-prepare are correct, and d is the impression of m;
  • it is in watch v;
  • it has not accepted a pre-prepare for watch v and number n with a different impression;
  • n lies between a low tide-mark h and a high tide-mark H — so a lying warden cannot exhaust the numbers by choosing an enormous one.

4.2 · Prepare: Did Everyone Hear the Same Thing?

A backup that accepts the pre-prepare sends ⟨PREPARE, v, n, d, i⟩ to all and logs both letters. Keepers accept prepares with correct seals, the current watch, and n between the tide-marks.

The Predicate prepared(m, v, n, i)

True if and only if keeper i has logged the request m, a pre-prepare for m in watch v with number n, and 2f prepares from different backups that match it (same watch, number and impression).

Invariant. If prepared(m, v, n, i) is true, then prepared(m′, v, n, j) is false for any honest j (including i) and any m′ with a different impression.

Why two different requests cannot both prepare with the same number
  1. prepared(m, v, n, i) means 2f + 1 keepers (the warden's pre-prepare plus 2f prepares) vouched for m at (v, n). With n = 3f + 1 keepers, at least f + 1 of them are honest.
  2. Likewise for m′. Two sets of 2f + 1 among 3f + 1 keepers share at least f + 1 keepers, so they share at least one honest keeper.
  3. That honest keeper would have sent two conflicting prepares (or pre-prepares) for the same watch and number. Honest keepers never do. And the chance that two different requests share an impression is negligible. ∎

This is why the gauntlet has one more round than the Synod of Volume IV. There, the ballot leader was trusted not to propose two different decrees under one ballot. Here, the warden may be the liar, and the prepare round is how the keepers establish that a quorum heard the identical proposal.

4.3 · Commit: Do Enough of Us Know It?

When prepared becomes true, keeper i sends ⟨COMMIT, v, n, D(m), i⟩ to all. Keepers accept properly sealed commits in their current watch with n in range.

committed and committed-local

committed(m, v, n) is true iff prepared(m, v, n, i) is true for every i in some set of f + 1 honest keepers.

committed-local(m, v, n, i) is true iff prepared(m, v, n, i) is true and i has accepted 2f + 1 matching commits (possibly including its own).

Invariant. If committed-local(m, v, n, i) is true for some honest i, then committed(m, v, n) is true.

A keeper carries out request m once it is committed-local and all requests with lower numbers have been carried out, and then replies to the citizen. It discards requests whose timestamp is lower than the last reply it sent that citizen. Because messengers may arrive out of order, a keeper may commit requests out of order — this does not matter, since it keeps their letters logged until they can be carried out in order.

Chapter V

Tide-Marks on the Harbour Wall

Letters cannot be kept for ever, but a keeper may discard them only when it knows the requests they concern have been carried out by at least f + 1 honest keepers — and can prove it to others during a watch change. A keeper who has missed letters that everyone else has discarded must be brought up to date by copying the treasury itself, and needs proof that the copy is honest.

Proofs after every request would be expensive, so keepers make them periodically: whenever a request with a number divisible by some constant (say 100) is carried out, the resulting state is a checkpoint. The keeper sends ⟨CHECKPOINT, n, d, i⟩, with d the impression of the whole treasury. Once a keeper holds 2f + 1 such letters from different keepers with the same n and d, the checkpoint is stable, and the keeper discards all pre-prepares, prepares and commits numbered n or less, and all earlier checkpoints.

The stable checkpoint sets the tide-marks. The low mark h is its number; the high mark is H = h + k, with k large enough that keepers do not stall waiting for the next checkpoint — if checkpoints come every 100 requests, k might be 200.

Chapter VI

Deposing the Warden

The gauntlet secures honest decisions. It does nothing about a lying warden who simply proposes nothing. The watch change is what makes the citadel live rather than merely refuse to lie. A backup starts a timer when it receives a valid request it has not yet carried out, and stops it when it is no longer waiting.

The Watch Change
  1. If backup i's timer expires in watch v, it stops accepting letters other than checkpoints and watch-change letters, and sends ⟨VIEW-CHANGE, v + 1, n, 𝒞, 𝒫, i⟩: n is its last stable checkpoint, 𝒞 the 2f + 1 checkpoint letters proving it, and 𝒫 a set containing, for each request that prepared at i above n, its pre-prepare and 2f matching prepares.
  2. When the warden of watch v + 1 has 2f valid VIEW-CHANGE letters from other keepers, it sends ⟨NEW-VIEW, v + 1, 𝒱, 𝒪⟩, where 𝒱 holds those letters and its own, and 𝒪 is a set of new pre-prepares computed as follows.
  3. Let min-s be the latest stable checkpoint in 𝒱 and max-s the highest number in any prepare in 𝒱. For each number between them: if some VIEW-CHANGE shows a request prepared with that number, re-propose ⟨PRE-PREPARE, v + 1, n, d⟩ with the impression from the highest watch in which it prepared; otherwise, propose a null request3 — a blank tablet that goes through the gauntlet and does nothing.
  4. A backup accepts the NEW-VIEW if it is properly sealed, its VIEW-CHANGE letters are valid, and 𝒪 is what it would itself compute from them. It then sends prepares for everything in 𝒪 and enters watch v + 1. Keepers redo the gauntlet for these numbers but do not re-execute requests they already carried out.
3. The null request, the inquisitors note, is a technique the Paxon Parliament also used to fill gaps in its ledger (Volume V, §3). A missing request body or checkpoint is fetched from another keeper; since f + 1 of the keepers who certified a checkpoint are honest, a keeper always obtains it or a later certified one.
Chapter VII

Why Nothing Is Lost

7.1 · Safety

The citadel is safe if all honest keepers agree on the numbers of requests that commit locally. Within one watch, the prepare invariant ensures it. Across watches, the watch change ensures it.

Sketch: a request committed in an earlier watch survives
  1. If m commits locally at an honest keeper with number n in watch v, then committed(m, v, n) holds: some set R1 of f + 1 honest keepers has prepared it.
  2. An honest keeper will not accept a pre-prepare for a later watch v′ without a NEW-VIEW for v′, and any correct NEW-VIEW contains valid VIEW-CHANGE letters from a set R2 of 2f + 1 keepers.
  3. With 3f + 1 keepers, R1 and R2 share at least one keeper k, and k is honest.
  4. k's VIEW-CHANGE carries the proof that m prepared at n — unless it reports a stable checkpoint beyond n. In the first case the new warden must re-propose m at n, preventing any other request from ever committing there. In the second, no keeper in the new watch will accept anything numbered n or lower. Either way, the keepers agree on what commits at n. ∎

7.2 · Liveness

To be live, keepers must change watches when they cannot make progress, but must keep 2f + 1 honest keepers in the same watch long enough — and ever longer — for a request to get through. The inquisitors use three means:

Three Means of Progress
  1. Patience that doubles. A keeper that sent VIEW-CHANGE for v + 1 waits for 2f + 1 such letters, then starts a timer of length T. If no NEW-VIEW arrives or no new request executes before it expires, it moves to v + 2 and this time waits 2T; then 4T; and so on.
  2. Not too late. A keeper that receives f + 1 valid VIEW-CHANGE letters for higher watches joins the smallest of them, even if its own timer has not expired.
  3. Liars cannot force chaos. A lying keeper cannot cause a watch change by itself, since one needs f + 1 keepers; it can stall only while it is warden. Because the warden's office rotates, the warden can be faulty for at most f consecutive watches.

These guarantee progress unless messengers' delays grow faster than the timeouts indefinitely — the navigators' passable season of Volume III, in the inquisitors' own terms.

7.3 · Uncertain Values

Some operations need a value that is not determined by the request — the library records "when this scroll was last amended", and each keeper's water-clock differs. The citizen cannot choose it, since he does not know how his request will be ordered. So the warden chooses, and the value travels through the gauntlet with the request, so that a lying warden cannot give different keepers different values. But a lying warden could give everyone the same bad value, so keepers must be able to judge it from the treasury alone. Where keepers must share in the choice, the warden gathers 2f + 1 sealed proposals and they take, say, the median.

Chapter VIII

The Budget

8.1 · Split Tallies Instead of Signets

Chapter IV described the gauntlet with signet seals on every letter. In fact the citadel uses signets only for VIEW-CHANGE and NEW-VIEW letters, which are rare, and authenticates all other letters with split tallies. That single change removes what the inquisitors identify as the main bottleneck of earlier fortifications.

The saving is enormous. On the inquisitors' machines, making a 1024-bit RSA signature of an MD5 digest took 43 ms and verifying it 0.6 ms; computing a tally over a 64-byte message took 10.3 µs — three orders of magnitude less. Each pair of keepers (and each active citizen) shares a 16-byte secret key; a tally is MD5 over the message and the key, keeping only the 10 least significant bytes. A reply needs one tally, for its one recipient. Every other letter carries an authenticator: a bundle with one tally for each other keeper. An authenticator is smaller than a 1024-bit RSA signature for systems of up to 13 keepers (tolerating 4 faults).

But a tally has a fundamental limit: it cannot prove a letter's authenticity to a third keeper. The gauntlet as written, and every earlier Byzantine fortification, relied on that power of signets. The inquisitors modified the protocol to do without it, using specific invariants — such as that no two different requests prepare with the same watch and number at two honest keepers — and give the full modification in a separate report.

8.2 · Fewer and Smaller Letters

Three Economies of Communication
  1. Impressions instead of large replies. A citizen names one keeper to send the full result; the others send only its impression. If the full reply is wrong, the citizen asks again for full replies from everyone.
  2. Tentative execution. Keepers carry out a request as soon as it is prepared and all earlier requests are committed, and send a tentative reply. A citizen with 2f + 1 matching tentative replies knows the request will commit. This cuts the message delays from 5 to 4. A tentative execution replaced by a null request in a watch change is rolled back to a checkpoint.
  3. Read-only requests. A citizen sends a read-only request to all keepers, who carry it out at once on their tentative state but reply only once that state is committed. The citizen needs 2f + 1 matching replies; if concurrent writes prevent that, he retries as an ordinary request. One round trip.

Under heavy load, the warden also gathers requests into batches and runs the gauntlet once for the batch.

Chapter IX

The Working Library

The inquisitors then did what no geometer had: they built a working library on the citadel and let the city use it. Their library of scrolls — a replicated version of the city's ordinary shared scroll-store — was reached by citizens exactly like the ordinary one, through a small relay that passed each request into the citadel. Behind the relay ran a library of their protocol and, in each of four keepers' houses, a simple scroll-keeper working on a single memory-mapped file.

Checkpoints were made cheap by copy-on-write: at each checkpoint every 512-byte page is marked, and a page is copied aside only when first changed. At most two checkpoints are kept; in their trials a checkpoint record averaged only 182 pages. The treasury's impression is computed incrementally with AdHash: the impression is the sum of the MD5 impressions of each page with its index, modulo a large number, so only changed pages need rehashing. The uncertain "last-modified" times were chosen by the warden, and keepers took the larger of that and one more than any earlier choice.4

4. The inquisitors report candidly that their library did not yet implement watch changes or retransmissions, and argue this does not affect their measurements, which were of the ordinary case, since the full algorithm had been formalized and proved correct.

9.1 · The Trials

All trials used four keepers — enough to survive one traitor, which the inquisitors expected to suffice for most purposes — on identical workstations joined by a 10 Mbit/s switched network, with checkpoints every 128 requests.

Even so, by a conservative estimate the citadel reduced latency relative to the Rampart by factors of more than 10 for read-write and 20 for read-only requests. In the trial of the five labours (the Andrew benchmark) — make the shelves, copy a tree of scrolls, check every label, read every word, bind the codices — the guarded library took 64.48 seconds against 51.07 for the same library without replication (26% more), and against 62.52 for the city's everyday scroll-store, which writes to its strongroom before replying: only 3% slower. With lookups treated as read-only, it was 2% faster than the everyday store. The guarded library replied faster in the labours with many writes, because it achieved durability by keeping copies instead of waiting on disk.

And then there is the bug. While running their trials, the inquisitors encountered a software error that struck their keepers independently — and the citadel continued running correctly in spite of it.

Chapter X

Rival Fortifications

The granary of Paxos (Volume VI) and the Paxon Parliament (Volume IV) already used watches with a warden and backups — but only against honest failures. Tolerating liars, the inquisitors observe, requires authentication, an extra pre-prepare phase, and a different way of triggering watch changes and choosing wardens. And the citadel uses watch changes only to choose a new warden, never to choose a different set of keepers.

The Rampart and the Secure Ring must expel faulty keepers to make progress and to tidy their logs, and decide who is faulty with failure detectors — which cannot be accurate in an asynchronous sea. A single misclassification can remove an honest keeper, which opens exactly the siege by delay of Chapter I. Calibrating the detectors to be patient enough for that risk to be negligible would leave the city waiting a very long time whenever the warden really does fall.

The Phalanx of Μάλκι and Ράιτερ — an appropriately Greek name — uses Byzantine quorums instead of a replicated state machine. It offers only reads, writes and locks on individual variables, needs 4f + 1 keepers, and a full copy of the treasury at each; its promise of scalability is real but expensive.

The inquisitors close with future work: fewer keepers, by using witnesses that join only when a full keeper fails — the same economy the granary had proposed — and fewer full copies of the treasury.

PBFT is the ancestor of essentially every practical Byzantine fault-tolerant replication protocol, including the many consensus protocols later built for permissioned blockchains. Its lasting design lessons are that safety must not depend on timing; that n = 3f + 1 replicas with quorums of 2f + 1 guarantee that any two quorums share an honest replica; that a view change must carry certificates proving what may have committed; and that cryptography is a budget item — signatures where proof to third parties is essential, MACs everywhere else.
